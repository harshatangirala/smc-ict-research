Do Smart Money Concepts actually beat the market — or just the market itself?

A full translation of two LuxAlgo Pine Script indicators (Smart Money Concepts, ICT Concepts) into Python, run as event detectors across 501 S&P 500 stocks and backtested at 8 holding horizons. The headline result: the aggregate claim doesn't hold up — but a real, specific minority of signals do.

01

Do SMC/ICT concepts outperform random entries and standard technical strategies?

Not uniformly, and the gap between the two tests above is the finding.

Every one of the 42 translated signal families — Break of Structure, Order Blocks, Fair Value Gaps, Change of Character, Liquidity Sweeps, and the rest, across both the ICT and SMC engines — produced a mean forward return significantly different from zero. On its own, that number is close to meaningless: 2010–2026 was a long bull market, and almost any signal biased toward long entries will show a positive average return purely from broad drift, independent of whether the signal contains real information.

So every ranking in this study was re-run against a second, harder bar: a random-entry strategy, backtested through identical mechanics, at the same trading frequency, on the same stocks, over the same window. Against that bar, only 28 of 42 concepts remain significant after correcting for testing 42 hypotheses at once (Benjamini–Hochberg FDR, α = 0.05).

Slice it by sector or market regime (Q5) and the picture gets more sober still: only 2 of 12 sectors, and one narrow regime slice, show the signal population beating the baseline at all. The unconditional claim "SMC/ICT signals beat chance" is not supported by this dataset. Where an edge exists, it is specific — to a concept, a sector, a regime — not a property of the methodology as a whole.

06

Which concepts fail consistently?

Every bearish-direction signal in the study — ict_bos_bearish, ict_fvg_bearish_filled, smc_fvg_bearish_formed, and the rest — shows a negative average forward return, significantly so. Read carefully: because these are short-biased signals, a negative return means the position lost money, i.e. price kept rising after the bearish signal fired. Across a 16-year bull market, that is a coherent, honest finding rather than a bug: these signals were systematically fighting the prevailing trend.

07

How stable is this across time and conditions?

Not very. The regime grid in Q5 has one positive cell out of nine. The one bright spot — bull markets during high-volatility stretches — is a narrow slice of the full trade population (180,610 of ~4.3M trades, about 5%). Every other combination of trend and volatility regime shows the signal population trailing a plain random entry. This is the most important caveat on the "28 concepts beat baseline" headline: that result is a 2010–2026, largely-bull-market average, and it does not appear to hold evenly across conditions.

08

Method, in brief

  • Translation. Both Pine scripts were read line by line and every concept ported to Python by hand — pivot detection, order block formation and mitigation, fair value gaps, liquidity pools, structure breaks — with overlapping vocabulary between the two engines (three different working definitions of "BOS," for instance) kept as separate signal families rather than merged.
  • Detection. Every detector ran against 501 of 503 S&P 500 constituents, daily bars, 2010–2026: 4,348,698 discrete events across 42 signal families.
  • Backtest. Every event was carried forward 1, 2, 3, 5, 10, 20, 40, and 60 trading days — 34,632,327 trades total. Entry uses only the signal bar's own close; every exit price and adverse/favorable excursion is computed strictly on bars after entry. No look-ahead by construction.
  • The baseline correction. A first pass reported all 42 concepts as "significant" — using only a zero-return null. That was a red flag, not a result: it meant a benchmark comparison was missing, not that the signals worked. A random-entry baseline was built and backtested through the identical pipeline, and every ranking — concepts, combinations, individual stocks, sectors, regimes — was re-tested against it. The numbers on this page are all post-correction.
  • Significance. Bootstrap confidence intervals, two-sample significance tests against the baseline, and Benjamini–Hochberg false-discovery-rate correction throughout, at α = 0.05.

Two concepts referenced in general ICT literature — Rejection Blocks and Optimal Trade Entry — have no corresponding logic in either source script and were not implemented. No transaction costs or slippage are modeled; these are in-sample results across the full study window, not a walk-forward or held-out test. Source indicators © LuxAlgo, CC BY-NC-SA 4.0.
