Do Smart Money Concepts actually beat the market — or just the market itself?

A full translation of two LuxAlgo Pine Script indicators (Smart Money Concepts, ICT Concepts) into Python, run as event detectors across 501 S&P 500 stocks and backtested at 8 holding horizons, with standard errors clustered by ticker. The headline result: none of it holds up — not the aggregate claim, and not one of the 42 individual concepts either, once tested fairly.

01

Do SMC/ICT concepts outperform random entries and standard technical strategies?

No — cleanly no, and the gap between the two tests above is the reason the aggregate result looked different at first.

Every one of the 42 translated signal families — Break of Structure, Order Blocks, Fair Value Gaps, Change of Character, Liquidity Sweeps, and the rest, across both the ICT and SMC engines — produced a mean forward return significantly different from zero. On its own, that number is close to meaningless: 2010–2026 was a long bull market, and almost any signal biased toward long entries will show a positive average return purely from broad drift, independent of whether the signal contains real information.

So every ranking in this study is re-run against a second, harder bar: a random-entry strategy, backtested through identical mechanics, at the same trading frequency, on the same stocks, over the same window, with standard errors clustered by ticker rather than treated as independent per-trade draws (trades on the same stock share overlapping holding windows and are not independent observations — see Q8). Against that bar, 35 of 42 concepts differ significantly from the baseline after correcting for testing 42 hypotheses at once (Benjamini–Hochberg FDR, α = 0.05) — but every one of those 35 differs in the negative direction. Zero concepts beat the baseline.

Slice it by sector or market regime (Q5) and the picture doesn't soften: 0 of 12 sectors, and 0 of 9 regime cells, show the signal population beating the baseline with statistical significance — and unlike the earlier cut of this study, not even the point estimates are mostly positive: 11 of 12 sectors and 8 of 9 regime cells have a negative excess return outright. The unconditional claim "SMC/ICT signals beat chance" is not supported by this dataset, and neither is the narrower claim that a specific minority of concepts do. Where the data comes closest to a positive result (Q2), it stops well short of significance.

The two order-block mitigation variants — price returning to tap a block after it forms — come closest, with small positive excess over baseline. One concept does reach significance in this table's neighborhood, in the wrong direction: ict_ndog_formed fires 1,941,685 times — by far the largest sample of any signal in the study — and that sample size is enough to make its small negative excess return (−0.13%) statistically significant (p = 0.030) even though the effect itself is tiny. Large samples make small effects visible; they don't make them large.

Which concept combinations are most robust?

268 same-direction concept pairs co-occur on the same stock and day at least 30 times; the 60 most frequent were tested. 0 of 60 beat the baseline after correction, and confluence doesn't rescue the picture: 56 of 60 are significantly worse than baseline — a slightly higher failure rate than single concepts (35 of 42), not the "confluence adds information" story a naive reading of the raw win rates would suggest.

06

Which concepts fail consistently?

Every bearish-direction signal in the study — ict_bos_bearish, ict_fvg_bearish_filled, smc_fvg_bearish_formed, and the rest — shows a negative average forward return, significantly so. Read carefully: because these are short-biased signals, a negative return means the position lost money, i.e. price kept rising after the bearish signal fired. Across a 16-year bull market, that is a coherent, honest finding rather than a bug: these signals were systematically fighting the prevailing trend.

Long-biased signals fail differently, and it's worth being precise about how. smc_fvg_bullish_formed (+0.65% raw), ict_ndog_formed (+0.71% raw), and ict_mss_bullish (+0.67% raw) all have positive raw returns and are significantly worse than the baseline — the raw number and the honest comparison point in opposite directions. ict_nwog_formed is the one exception among long-biased signals that's genuinely indistinguishable from baseline rather than significantly worse (p = 0.20). A positive raw return with no baseline comparison is not evidence of an edge — it can just as easily describe a signal actively working against you at a smaller loss than the market's average gain.

07

How stable is this across time and conditions?

No. The regime grid in Q5 has one positive cell out of nine — bear markets during low-volatility stretches — and it is both statistically insignificant (p = 0.90) and a narrow slice of the data (15,569 of 4.3M trades in this cut, 0.4%). Every other combination of trend and volatility regime shows the signal population trailing a plain random entry, several by more than a full percentage point. This is the sharper version of the "0 of 42 concepts beat baseline" headline, not a separate caveat on it: there is no regime, sector, or time slice in this dataset where the aggregate signal population comes out ahead with statistical support.

08

Method, in brief

  • Translation. Both Pine scripts were read line by line and every concept ported to Python by hand — pivot detection, order block formation and mitigation, fair value gaps, liquidity pools, structure breaks — with overlapping vocabulary between the two engines (three different working definitions of "BOS," for instance) kept as separate signal families rather than merged.
  • Detection. Every detector ran against 501 of 503 S&P 500 constituents, daily bars, 2010–2026: 4,326,101 discrete events across 42 signal families.
  • Backtest. Every event was carried forward 1, 2, 3, 5, 10, 20, 40, and 60 trading days — 34,452,258 trades total. Entry uses only the signal bar's own close; every exit price and adverse/favorable excursion is computed strictly on bars after entry. No look-ahead by construction.
  • The baseline correction. A first pass reported all 42 concepts as "significant" — using only a zero-return null. That was a red flag, not a result: it meant a benchmark comparison was missing, not that the signals worked. A random-entry baseline was built and backtested through the identical pipeline, and every ranking — concepts, combinations, individual stocks, sectors, regimes — was re-tested against it.
  • Two further corrections, applied after the first published cut of this study. (1) The per-ticker random baseline was seeded from a hash of each ticker's first cached bar date; because most tickers share that date, roughly 84% of the universe was drawing the same "random" dates rather than independent ones, and the fix (a stable per-ticker seed) changed the baseline meaningfully — it's the reason the sector table in Q5 no longer has any positive entries. (2) Every significance test now clusters standard errors by ticker rather than treating each trade as an independent draw: trades on the same stock share overlapping holding-period windows, and a naive per-trade test understates the true standard error as sample size grows. Sector and market-regime rankings did not carry a significance test at all in the first published cut — a raw excess-return number with no test of whether it differs from noise — and now do.
  • Also fixed: the SMC order-block detector had its bullish/bearish extreme-candle selection swapped relative to the source script (picking the highest high for bullish blocks and the lowest low for bearish ones, the reverse of the source's demand/supply logic), and both engines' order-block detectors included the current breakout bar in their search window where the source excludes it. Both are corrected; the numbers on this page reflect the corrected detectors.
  • Significance. Cluster-robust confidence intervals, cluster-robust two-sample significance tests against the baseline, and Benjamini–Hochberg false-discovery-rate correction throughout, at α = 0.05. Clustering corrects for within-ticker correlation; it is not two-way (ticker-by-date) clustered, so cross-ticker correlation on shared market-wide dates is not separately corrected for — a conservative gap, since implementing it would tighten these results further, not loosen them.

Two concepts referenced in general ICT literature — Rejection Blocks and Optimal Trade Entry — have no corresponding logic in either source script and were not implemented. No transaction costs or slippage are modeled; these are in-sample results across the full study window, not a walk-forward or held-out test. The universe reflects current-day S&P 500 membership applied retroactively across 2010–2026, not a point-in-time-correct historical constituent list — constituents removed from the index during the window are absent, and recent additions carry their full available trading history despite not having been index members for most of it.
